# Research Method Router

Read only the modules that fit the assignment. Record the selected method in the plan so source selection, coverage, and stopping rules match the kind of claim being made.

## Systematic or scoping review

Use when the user asks what a body of literature shows, requests a systematic/scoping review, or needs reproducible coverage.

- Write a protocol before searching: question framework, databases or source systems, date/language/geography limits, source types, inclusion/exclusion rules, deduplication method, and synthesis method.
- Translate the question into concept blocks and controlled vocabulary where the database supports it. Preserve exact queries, dates, filters, and result counts by source system.
- Deduplicate before screening. Track records identified, screened, excluded with reason, sought, unavailable, and included.
- Separate title/abstract screening from full-text eligibility. Do not change eligibility criteria silently after seeing results; record and justify amendments.
- Assess study quality or risk of bias using a framework appropriate to the field. Do not treat publication count as evidentiary weight.
- Use narrative synthesis unless study designs, measures, and populations justify quantitative pooling. Never imply meta-analysis from informal averaging.
- Stop at protocol-defined coverage or documented saturation, not at the first stable narrative.

## Empirical and quantitative research

Use for statistics, datasets, experiments, observational studies, surveys, forecasts, causal claims, or market measurements.

- Record the population, unit of analysis, sample, period, geography, variable definitions, denominator, missing-data treatment, and data provenance.
- Distinguish descriptive, associational, causal, predictive, and normative claims. Match the written claim to the design actually used.
- Inspect identification strategy, comparison group, measurement validity, uncertainty intervals, model specification, preregistration when relevant, and robustness or sensitivity tests.
- Check whether reported values are nominal/real, seasonally adjusted, weighted, revised, or based on incompatible definitions.
- Prefer original datasets and methods appendices. Recompute simple quantities when feasible and preserve the formula or query.
- Treat statistical significance, effect size, practical importance, and generalizability as separate questions.
- Search for replications, corrections, retractions, data revisions, and credible alternative explanations.

## Historical or archival research

Use when claims depend on change over time, historical meaning, institutional development, or an incomplete archive.

- Define the periodization, geography, institutions, actors, and source survival problem before searching.
- Build an epoch-and-transition matrix before drafting. For each epoch record its date range, governing category or framework, representative evidence, counterexamples, transition markers, and the proposition the record can support.
- When several legal or institutional conceptions coexist, build a separate conceptions taxonomy: conception or model, core inquiry, representative sources, source role, epochs in which it appears, and present significance. Use the taxonomy to organize the chronology rather than treating one source as one self-contained doctrine.
- State a boundary policy for materials just outside the requested period or category: exclude them, use them as an epilogue, or use them only as transition evidence. Do not silently count boundary sources as in-period proof.
- Search period vocabulary, spelling variants, predecessor concepts, names, and adjacent institutions rather than relying only on modern terms.
- Record provenance, genre, intended audience, institutional position, publication context, and whether a source describes rule, practice, argument, or aspiration.
- Triangulate across source genres and seek exceptions, silences, and marginalized actors. Do not turn archival absence into proof of nonexistence.
- Test negative space and selection effects: what the archive preserves, what the chosen source universe excludes, whether canonical sources are being mistaken for representative ones, and whether assumed baselines or unlitigated practices evade direct treatment.
- Where legal or institutional categories distributed protection or vulnerability unevenly, ask whose injuries, exclusions, or experiences the surviving authorities foreground or obscure. Include this analysis only when the evidence makes it material.
- Reconcile relevant prior local reports through an authority/claim delta before broad retrieval: identify reused sources, omitted authorities, conflicting classifications, and genuinely new evidence. Verify rather than inherit their conclusions.
- Allocate discovery and deep-read coverage across the declared epochs, transitions, and conceptions. Rank candidates in interleaved rounds so budget trimming reduces depth within cells before erasing a cell entirely.
- Separate source content from the researcher’s inference and later interpretation. Avoid projecting present-day categories backward.

## Comparative or cross-jurisdiction research

Use for countries, jurisdictions, markets, institutions, cultures, or products whose categories may not be equivalent.

- Define the unit of comparison and the reason each case belongs in the comparison.
- Create a shared concept table: local term, translation, formal definition, functional equivalent, time period, and important mismatch.
- Search in relevant local languages when feasible and use authoritative local sources; disclose language limits and dependence on translations.
- Compare the same level of authority and implementation: law with law, practice with practice, announced policy with announced policy.
- Explain selection effects, missing cases, institutional differences, and whether a result travels beyond the compared set.

## Fast-moving events and current-state research

Use for news, active litigation or regulation, product availability, prices, officeholders, incidents, and other facts likely to change.

- Record both publication time and event/effective time. Prefer the most recent authoritative update, but retain earlier versions when they explain change.
- Build a short chronology and distinguish proposal, announcement, enactment, implementation, correction, and current status.
- Check official statements or records against at least one independent source when incentives or reporting errors matter.
- Treat live pages, dashboards, and rolling datasets as versioned evidence; capture the access time and relevant value.
- State the cutoff prominently and avoid future-tense certainty about scheduled or proposed events.

## Method handoff

If a domain-specific skill supplies stricter rules, preserve the claim/source ledgers and hand them off rather than duplicating the entire search. Record which claims still need domain review.
