# Memo Architecture and House Style

Use this as the default drafting template. Adapt the headings to the assignment; do not include empty sections merely to follow the template.

## Recommended structure

### Title and metadata

Use a descriptive title. Include author, date, recipient if supplied, and “Research current through [date].” If the source set is closed, say “Based on the materials supplied” and identify the cutoff.

For project artifacts, prefer a stable lowercase slug plus cutoff date (`topic-short-name-YYYY-MM-DD.ext`) unless the project has another convention. Record the exact path in the research-run manifest so authorship and provenance can be reconstructed without session-log archaeology.

### Question Presented

State one answerable question containing the relevant jurisdiction, legal rule, and material facts. Split compound questions into numbered questions when the answers differ.

### Short Answer / Executive Summary

Lead with the bottom line in one or two paragraphs. Identify the strongest authority, the central reason, and the principal caveat. Avoid burying the answer under background.

### Facts, assumptions, and scope

Separate supplied facts from assumptions and disputed or missing facts. Explain exclusions: jurisdictions not searched, dates not covered, procedural questions not addressed, or sources not available.

### Governing framework

Introduce the controlling text and doctrinal test. Define terms that are used inconsistently. Put the rule in the level of generality supported by the authorities.

### Analysis

Use descriptive headings that state the analytical move, such as:

- “The controlling cases treat the issue as a question of …”
- “The strongest authority supports X, but the later cases narrow that principle to …”
- “The historical record supports the rule’s existence, not necessarily its modern scope.”

For each section, use this sequence when it fits:

1. short answer;
2. rule and best authority;
3. leading applications;
4. adverse authority or competing interpretation;
5. application to the supplied facts; and
6. confidence and unresolved point.

### Counterarguments and limits

Give the best opposing argument fairly. Identify whether it is supported by binding precedent, persuasive authority, text, historical evidence, a separate opinion, or an inference. Explain the answer’s vulnerability rather than dismissing the objection.

### Conclusion and next steps

Restate the answer briefly. Identify the single most useful missing fact, authority, or procedural development. Do not add generic recommendations that do not follow from the research.

## Style rules

- Prefer a synthesis paragraph over a paragraph for every case.
- Introduce a case by explaining why it matters, then give only the facts and reasoning needed for the proposition.
- Use quotations where exact language is legally significant, historically revealing, or difficult to paraphrase; otherwise paraphrase with a pinpoint.
- Distinguish a court’s holding from its description of an argument, a hypothetical, dictum, or a dissent.
- Use chronology only when it explains doctrinal development. For historical research, combine chronology with conceptual headings so the reader can see both change and continuity.
- Make uncertainty visible through precise qualifiers: “likely,” “the better reading,” “the cases divide,” “the record is thin,” or “the question remains open.” Avoid vague hedging.
- Keep footnotes useful: cite the authority for the proposition, add a short explanatory parenthetical when it clarifies the relevance, and avoid turning footnotes into an uncited second memo.

## Optional authority table

Include an authority table when the memo turns on several cases or a split:

| Authority | Court/date | Proposition | Force | Limitation or adverse point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Case or source | Court and year | What it actually establishes | Binding/persuasive/etc. | Distinction, contrary language, or later treatment |

Use the table to orient the reader, not to replace the analysis.

## Verification disclosure

For deep, scholarly, or high-stakes work, append a compact table listing each material authority, verification level, and unresolved limitation. For standard work, include the table only when levels are mixed or a material authority was not checked in full. For targeted work, use a short inline or closing caveat. Do not let disclosure machinery overwhelm the legal analysis.
