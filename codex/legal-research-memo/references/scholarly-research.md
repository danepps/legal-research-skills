# Scholarly Research Mode

Use this file for law-review articles, dissertation chapters, monographs, intellectual history, legal history, legal theory, conceptual genealogy, and other projects whose goal is scholarly explanation or intervention rather than a predictive legal answer.

## Reframe the research problem

Begin with a research problem, not only a question presented. A useful problem statement identifies:

- the phenomenon, concept, doctrine, institution, or vocabulary to be explained;
- the period, jurisdiction, legal tradition, or archive in scope;
- the received account or scholarly disagreement;
- the evidence that may complicate that account; and
- the claim the project might responsibly make.

Keep the thesis provisional during discovery. Distinguish a descriptive claim, causal claim, genealogical claim, normative claim, and methodological claim. Do not call an observation an intervention until the relevant literature has been mapped.

## Build a historiography and literature map

Organize secondary sources by argument, method, period, and evidence—not just by author or publication date. For each major work, record:

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Argument | The work’s central claim in its own terms |
| Evidence | Cases, statutes, archives, institutions, interviews, or data used |
| Method | Doctrinal, historical, sociological, intellectual-history, comparative, theoretical, etc. |
| Scope | Period, jurisdiction, actors, and exclusions |
| Contribution | What the work explains or changes |
| Limitation | Evidence or assumption that leaves room for another account |
| Relation to project | Extends, revises, contests, reframes, or supplies background |

Add a specificity field: `direct_answer`, `component_answer`, `adjacent_domain_analogy`, or `background`. Build and verify the direct-answer core before expanding the adjacent literature. Search the project’s exact formulation and close variants; inspect titles, abstracts, tables of contents, and headings; and citation-chase the closest work in both directions. Promote likely direct treatments to full text. Do not infer that a direct literature is absent from the silence of adjacent-source abstracts.

Synthesize the literature by problem or debate. Avoid a sequence of “Author A says … Author B says …” paragraphs unless the sequence itself demonstrates a change in the field.

## Construct a source base

Separate primary sources from secondary interpretation. Build a source inventory that records date, provenance, genre, author or institutional position, intended audience, legal status, and preservation or publication context. Possible primary materials include opinions, statutes, treatises, digests, briefs, legislative materials, judicial records, correspondence, institutional records, newspapers, administrative documents, and contemporary commentary.

Read sources against their grain as well as for their explicit claims. Ask who produced the source, for what purpose, under what institutional constraint, and whose experience or argument it may omit. Do not treat a judicial opinion, treatise, brief, or later scholarly account as a transparent description of social practice.

## Develop periodization and concepts

Choose periods because the legal problem, vocabulary, institution, or source environment changes—not only because of round dates. Define key terms at the time and place in which they were used. Track whether the same word changed meaning, whether different words named a similar practice, and whether a later court projected a modern category backward.

Use a concept table when terminology is unstable:

| Term or category | Period/jurisdiction | Who used it | Meaning in context | Later transformation |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Distinguish continuity in language from continuity in legal function. A doctrine may retain a label while changing its institutional role, justification, beneficiaries, or remedial consequences.

## Analyze evidence and rival accounts

For each major claim, present the evidence, the inference drawn from it, and the alternative explanations. Test the thesis against inconvenient cases, minority jurisdictions, failed proposals, dissenting voices, and sources that use different vocabulary. Explain whether an apparent absence reflects nonexistence, nonlitigation, nonpublication, archival loss, or a search limitation.

Separate what the sources establish from what the project infers. Use calibrated formulations such as “the evidence is consistent with,” “the record suggests,” “this supports a revision of,” or “this cannot establish.” Avoid universal claims from a narrow archive.

## Draft for scholarly contribution

The opening should tell the reader:

1. what the project explains;
2. what existing accounts miss or assume;
3. what sources and method make the argument possible;
4. what the project argues; and
5. why the argument matters for legal history, doctrine, theory, or institutional understanding.

Organize the body around the argument, not the order in which sources were found. Use sections to develop a sequence of claims. At the end of each major section, state what has been established and how it advances or complicates the thesis.

## Scholarly audit

Before delivery, confirm that:

- the thesis is distinguishable from the topic;
- the literature review represents the relevant debates fairly;
- the direct-answer core was searched, citation-chased, and distinguished from component, adjacent, and background sources;
- any claim that directly responsive scholarship was not located states the bounded searches, databases or source universe, cutoff, and access limits;
- primary and secondary sources are clearly separated;
- the archive or corpus is described, including selection criteria and gaps;
- historical actors are not treated as using present-day categories without evidence;
- chronology, causation, correlation, and conceptual similarity are not conflated;
- rival explanations receive substantive treatment;
- quotations and paraphrases are verified against the source;
- the claimed contribution is no broader than the evidence; and
- the conclusion states what the project changes, what it cannot show, and what further research would test it.
