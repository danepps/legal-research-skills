# Historical, Originalist, Common-Law, and General-Law Research

Use this protocol when the assignment asks what a legal term meant historically, whether a doctrine reflects general law, how a rule developed, or how older authorities bear on modern constitutional analysis.

## Define the historical claim

Separate the possible claims before searching:

- a rule was widely recognized;
- a term had a particular legal meaning;
- a rule was part of judge-made general law;
- a practice was accepted, contested, or exceptional;
- a later court adopted, rejected, transformed, or merely cited the older rule; or
- the historical evidence bears on constitutional meaning, modern doctrine, or only background.

Do not treat these claims as interchangeable. A handful of canonical cases may show that a rule existed without showing that it was universal, uncontested, or constitutionally fixed.

Claims using `first`, `earliest`, `only`, `most`, `overwhelmingly`, `always`, `never`, or equivalent language require a documented source universe and search protocol. If the available record cannot support that protocol, state an existence, illustrative, or bounded comparative claim instead.

## Build the historical record

Before broad searching, create an epoch-and-transition matrix. For each proposed epoch record its date range, governing category or framework, representative authorities, counterexamples, transition markers, and the proposition the evidence can support. State whether sources just outside the requested period are excluded, treated as an epilogue, or used only to explain a transition; do not silently count them as in-period evidence.

When the record contains multiple conceptions or models, build a separate taxonomy before writing the chronology: `conception or model | core inquiry | representative authorities | source role | epochs | present significance`. Use this taxonomy as an analytical spine so overlapping conceptions remain visible within an epoch and the chronology explains change rather than substituting for synthesis.

Search in layers:

1. constitutional or statutory text in force at the relevant time;
2. cases from the relevant period, including cases using different terminology;
3. treatises, digests, restatements, and practice manuals close to the period;
4. state-law variation and jurisdictional exceptions;
5. later cases explaining how the older authorities were understood; and
6. scholarship that identifies missing, conflicting, or revisionist evidence.

Track dates, jurisdiction, legal regime, procedural posture, source role, classification status, precedential effect, and whether the source describes law, custom, argument, or policy. Search for contrary examples and for cases that assume a rule without deciding it. Reconcile relevant prior local memoranda with an authority/claim delta before duplicating retrieval; verify rather than inherit their classifications or conclusions.

Assign stable coverage-cell IDs to the material epochs, transitions, conceptions, jurisdictions, or subquestions. Select deep reads in interleaved rounds—the best source for each material cell, then the second-best for each—so a budget reduction trims depth before erasing an entire part of the planned account. If a material cell still has no read authority, run one targeted retrieval when reserved budget permits; otherwise disclose the gap and do not mark planned coverage complete.

Test the record's negative space. Ask whether reported cases overrepresent contested interests, whether routine or assumed legal baselines escape litigation, whether canonical authorities are representative, and which jurisdictions, groups, or forms of injury the surviving archive makes visible or obscures. Where legal categories distributed protection or vulnerability unevenly, address historical harm or exclusion when the sources support it; do not add a generic social-history section untethered to the legal claim.

## Analyze continuity and change

Organize the memo so a reader can distinguish:

- the original rule or term;
- the reasons courts gave for it;
- the range of accepted exceptions;
- later doctrinal restatement or displacement;
- the modern court’s use of history; and
- the inference the historical record can and cannot support.

When older authorities use a modern-sounding word, do not assume they used it in the modern doctrinal sense. Quote the relevant language, identify the legal problem the court was solving, and compare neighboring doctrines that may explain the result.

## Treat absences carefully

An authority’s silence may mean that the issue was assumed, irrelevant to the judgment, not litigated, or omitted from the report. State which inference is supported. Do not convert a search failure or a court’s non-discussion into a historical rule.

## State the level of historical confidence

End historical sections by identifying whether the evidence is:

- strong and geographically broad;
- strong but limited to a legal tradition or period;
- mixed or contested;
- sparse but suggestive; or
- insufficient to support the requested claim.

Explain what additional source would most improve the conclusion.
